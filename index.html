01 — THE MATERIAL

What is a semiconductor?

Every material resists the flow of electricity by a different amount. A semiconductor sits in the narrow middle ground — not a free conductor, not a total blocker — and that in-between behavior is exactly what makes it controllable enough to build a transistor out of.

02 — FROM MATERIAL TO CHIP

What is VLSI?

VLSI — Very Large Scale Integration — is the practice of packing millions to billions of transistors made from silicon onto a single chip. Once you decide to build a chip, there are two very different roads to get there.

ASIC flow

A custom chip designed for one specific job, built from scratch and sent to a foundry to be fabricated. Slower and expensive to start, but faster and cheaper once you're making millions of units.

Spec→ RTL→ Synthesis→ DFT→ Physical→ Fab

FPGA flow

A pre-manufactured chip full of reconfigurable logic blocks that you programme to behave the way you want. No fabrication step — you can reprogram it in minutes, which makes it perfect for prototyping.

Spec→ RTL→ Synthesis→ Place & Route→ Bitstream→ Program device

This walkthrough follows the ASIC path, because that's where DFT — the subject of this whole page — becomes a distinct, dedicated stage of its own.

04 — WHY YOU'RE LEARNING THIS

What is DFT, and why does it matter?

Design for Test (DFT) is the practice of building extra circuitry into a chip — not to make it do its job, but to make it possible to prove it does its job after it's been manufactured. Verification proves the design is correct on paper. DFT proves the physical part that comes off the fab line is defect-free.

01

Manufacturing isn't perfect

Even a correct design can come out of the fab with a bridged wire, an open via, or a stuck transistor. DFT is how those individual chips get caught.

02

You can't functionally test everything

Running the chip's real workload can't reach every internal node in a reasonable amount of time. DFT gives direct, fast control and observation of internal logic.

03

It's what ships to the customer

Every unit that reaches a customer was tested through DFT structures on the tester floor — this is the last line of defense before shipping.

05 — FUNCTIONAL VERIFICATION VS. DFT

Same circuit, two very different questions.

Take a half adder: sum = a XOR b, carry = a AND b. Functional verification asks "is this the right logic?" DFT asks a completely different question: "was this exact chip, once it was manufactured, built correctly — defect free?"

Watch the signal move from a and b, through the XOR and AND gates, to sum and carry. This is functionality — the logic is correct.

CONCEPT 01

Scan insertion

Scan is what makes a flip-flop testable. Every normal flip-flop gets a 2-to-1 mux placed in front of it, so it can either take its regular functional input, or be chained into a long shift register that a tester can shift values through directly.

  • A regular D flip-flop only has a data input and a clock — no way to force an arbitrary value into it from outside.
  • Scan insertion adds a 2×1 mux ahead of the flop: input 0 is the normal functional path (D), input 1 is the scan-in path (SI).
  • The Scan Enable (SE) signal picks which one gets used — click the toggle in the diagram to switch modes.
  • Chaining SI → Q → SI across every flop in the design forms one long shift register: the scan chain.
  • With SE = 1, a tester can shift any pattern directly into every flop, then shift the results back out — full control and observation, without relying on the chip's normal function.
CONCEPT 02

Scan compression (EDT)

A modern SoC can have thousands of scan chains — far more than a tester has physical channels to drive. Scan compression embeds a decompressor and compactor around the chains so a small number of tester pins can still feed and read a huge number of chains.

  • Compression ratio = number of scan chains ÷ number of scan channels reaching the tester.
  • The decompressor fans a handful of tester channels out into hundreds or thousands of scan chains.
  • The compactor fans all those chain outputs back down to a small number of channels the tester can read.
  • Net effect: shorter test time, less test data volume, fewer chip-level pins spent on test, and lower ATE memory requirements.
